# Split tax rate research

**What it is:** a self-started research project at the Jefferson County PVA. Kentucky HB 607 (2026) authorizes Louisville Metro to adopt a split-rate property tax in the Urban Services District, so I built a study of how other places run split-roll and split-rate systems, and who to call to learn from them.

## What I built

- A spreadsheet of **36 jurisdictions in about 20 states and DC**, covering both kinds of split: residential versus commercial (split roll or classified) and land versus buildings (split rate or land value tax).
- For each place: its status (in use, authorized but not adopted, proposed, stalled, ended), what it does, why it matters to an assessor's office, sources, the assessor's office contact and the governing body's communications contact.
- Summary counts by status and a glossary of terms.
- I started outreach to assessors. Some replied with useful context, for example that only one city in their county uses a split rate.
- The purpose: so PVA leadership could meet these offices at the IAAO annual conference.

## What stood out

- The state fiscal note on HB 607 names higher administrative costs for the assessor's office for valuing land and improvements separately.
- **New Castle County, Delaware** is the closest example to a PVA crisis and the strongest communications case study: the first reassessment since 1983, huge swings in values, reissued bills, extended deadlines and a court challenge. School districts were allowed to set separate residential and nonresidential rates.
- **Pittsburgh** ended its split rate within weeks of reassessment notices going out in a heated election. The lesson: reassessment communications can decide the outcome.
- **Boston** shows how hard it is to explain a residential tax increase driven by falling commercial values. The city built a tax relief calculator.

The spreadsheet itself is not published here because it holds contact notes from my outreach. I am glad to walk through it.
